### Scientist 1

## Core claims
1. Water transport in RO/NF polyamide membranes is pressure-driven viscous pore flow through dynamic, interconnected subnanometre pores, not solution–diffusion through a homogeneous dense polymer.  
2. Ion transport and selectivity emerge from coupled partitioning and migration barriers—dehydration, Donnan/steric/dielectric exclusion, and ion–membrane friction—rather than from a single constant permeability coefficient.  
3. A predictive membrane theory must connect molecular simulations, nano-/ångström-scale model channels, operando characterization, and macroscopic transport models.

## Method framework
This is a perspective/synthesis, not a new experimental study. It compares macroscopic transport models—solution–diffusion, solution–diffusion–electromigration, DSPM-DE, and sorption–friction—with nanofluidic/angstrofluidic observations and molecular dynamics simulations of polyamide RO/NF membranes. The analysis is mainly one-dimensional coupon-scale transport, using feed composition and pressure or voltage as inputs and water/ion fluxes as outputs. Length scales range from Å/subnanometre pores, through nanometre channels and membrane free volume, to macroscopic membrane performance. MD evidence is typically nanometre-sized and nanosecond-to-microsecond, while discussed pressures include 50–80 bar for RO operation and 300–1,500 bar in simulations. Data sources include published MD/non-equilibrium MD, positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy, small-angle neutron scattering, wide-angle X-ray scattering, solvent permeation tests, and single-channel nanofluidic measurements.

## Key evidence
- **Macroscopic transport phenomena and models**: solution–diffusion is challenged by non-equilibrium MD, theory, and permeation experiments showing Darcy-type viscous flow and an internal pressure gradient; sorption–friction couples water and salt transport through water–ion and water–membrane friction.  
- **Macroscopic transport phenomena and models / Fig. 2**: DSPM-DE explicitly includes Donnan, steric, and dielectric partitioning plus extended Nernst–Planck migration, but relies on hard-sphere assumptions and ambiguous ion sizes; SF absorbs non-Donnan partitioning into an empirical coefficient and migration into friction factors.  
- **Microscopic behaviour in nano- and ångström-scale channels**: continuum equations remain useful down to single-digit nanometres but fail to capture sub-continuum friction, fluctuations, and confinement effects.  
- **Microscopic behaviour / Fig. 3**: sub-1-nm channels form single-file water chains with transport faster than bulk-viscosity predictions; ion mobility in ultra-narrow 2D slits scales with hydrated ion diameter; 0.8-nm metallic carbon nanotube porins show higher water permeability than semiconducting nanotubes of the same diameter.  
- **Molecular-level mechanisms in membranes and nanochannels**: MD shows polyamide free volume as irregular cavities with pore sizes about 0.3–0.6 nm, consistent with positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy; pores open, close, and merge on nanosecond timescales.  
- **Molecular-level mechanisms**: applying 300–600 bar slightly reduces fractional free volume but creates new percolation pathways; under steady flux, water concentration is uniform across the membrane while hydrostatic pressure drops linearly.  
- **Molecular-level mechanisms**: MD over 300–1,500 bar gives a linear flux–pressure relationship, supporting pressure-driven pore flow; water moves as clusters rather than isolated molecules.  
- **Molecular-level mechanisms**: ion permeabilities are typically 4–8 orders of magnitude lower than water permeability; ion entry requires partial dehydration costing tens of kcal/mol; at 50–80 bar, fully hydrated Na⁺ entry into a 0.5-nm pore is essentially zero unless 2–3 water molecules are lost.  
- **Molecular-level mechanisms**: ion migration is affected by binding to charged/polar polymer sites; Cl⁻ may partition more easily than F⁻ but diffuse more slowly inside, and Li⁺ can hop more readily than Na⁺/K⁺ in sulfonated pores due to weaker binding.  
- **Bridging molecular, microscopic and macroscopic scales / Outlook**: small-angle and very-small-angle neutron scattering support interconnected pore networks; physics-informed ML for multi-species ion transport can outperform traditional PDE solvers when physical constraints are embedded.

The source does not present new membrane experiments or simulations; it reviews and integrates existing work. It explicitly restricts macroscopic discussion to one-dimensional coupon-scale models and ignores concentration polarization and crossflow spatial distributions. It does not claim single nanofluidic channels can be directly multiplied to predict real membranes; it treats real polymer morphology, tortuosity, heterogeneity, mesoscale polarization, and nonlinear operation as unresolved. It does not solve the empirical parameter problem in sorption–friction or fully quantify dehydration, dielectric exclusion, or ion–ion pairing. It acknowledges MD limits: nanometre length, nanosecond-to-microsecond time, fixed-charge pairwise force fields lacking polarization and chemistry, small-cell boundary effects, and mostly simple single-salt feeds. It also notes limited MD work on ion-exchange membranes, lack of operando internal-pore observation, and absent scalable fabrication for ideal membranes.

# A multiscale perspective for understanding transport mechanisms in desalination and ion-selective membranes

*Hanqing Fan, Makenna Parkinson, Kumar Varoon Agrawal, Mihail Barboiu, Lydéric Bocquet, Bezawit A. Getachew, Qilin Li, Ying Li, Shihong Lin, Chong Liu, Aleksandr Noy, Boya Radha, Dietmar Schwahn, Anthony Szymczyk, Menachem Elimelech.* *Nature Water* (2026), Perspective. DOI: 10.1038/s44221-026-00585-1

## Abstract

Membranes with nanometre- and subnanometre-scale pores play a vital role in aqueous separations across applications ranging from desalination and wastewater reuse to resource recovery and green hydrogen production. Despite their widespread use, the molecular-level mechanisms that govern water and solute transport in these membranes remain inadequately understood. In this Perspective, we examine advances in membrane and nanochannel transport across macroscopic, microscopic and molecular scales to establish a unified mechanistic framework. We begin by analysing current macroscopic models, highlighting their simplifying assumptions and inherent limitations. We then explore insights from nano- and ångström-scale fluidic studies, revealing unconventional transport phenomena that are not captured by classical continuum theories. Next, we describe how molecular simulations offer atomistic resolution of transport processes, providing mechanistic insight into how water and ions traverse the dynamic, heterogeneous porous networks of real-world, state-of-the-art polymer membranes. Finally, we discuss how to integrate these molecular, microscopic and macroscopic scales to advance theoretical understanding and inform the rational design of next-generation membranes. We conclude by identifying key knowledge gaps and outlining emerging opportunities to bridge scales through advanced characterization techniques and multiscale modelling.

## Macroscopic transport phenomena and models

Macroscopic models for membrane transport are mathematical models that describe the physics of transport and generate water and ion fluxes as outputs, using feed solution composition and operating conditions as inputs. Feed composition refers to the types and concentrations of ions, whereas the key operating conditions include the applied pressure in RO/NF or the applied voltage or current density in electrodialysis. The model parameters include membrane properties and, when considering concentration polarization, the characteristics of the boundary layer. Here we focus our discussion on one-dimensional coupon-scale models, ignoring concentration polarization and spatial distributions along the crossflow direction. Our focus is on the fundamental mechanisms governing water and ion transport across ion-selective membranes. These mechanistic models serve as universal building blocks that can be adapted into process-specific mass transport models by incorporating appropriate membrane properties (for example, RO/NF membranes versus IEMs) and boundary conditions reflecting different driving forces (for example, pressure-driven RO/NF versus electrically driven electrodialysis).

The development of robust and accurate macroscopic models has practical importance beyond demonstrating in-depth fundamental understanding of the transport phenomena involved. First, a coupon-scale transport model is essential to module-scale modelling, which in turn is critical to process design and optimization. The ability of a model to accurately predict ion and water fluxes is particularly important to membrane processes for selective ion separation (for example, salt fractionation using NF or electrodialysis) and for membrane processes where ions are not perfectly rejected (for example, in low-salt-rejection RO). Second, coupon-scale transport models provide the theoretical framework for extracting membrane parameters to evaluate and compare membrane material performance. Ideally, the framework should be relatively simple with a small set of intrinsic parameters. Nature Water

### Water transport

For water transport, two major model frameworks have been proposed: SD and viscous pore flow. The SD model, proposed over 50 years ago, describes the water transport as a two-step process, with water molecules partitioning into a ‘dense’ polymer matrix as the first step and the diffusion of water molecules across the membrane (or the active separation layer in RO/NF membrane) as the second step (Fig. 2a). Recent non-equilibrium molecular dynamics simulations, theoretical analysis and permeation experiments disprove the underlying assumptions of the SD model, revealing a Darcy-type viscous flow with a pressure gradient and water (or solvent) transport as a continuum—behaviours consistent with the pore flow model (Fig. 2b,c).

Despite the popularity of the simple SD model in the past few decades, the pore flow model was proposed earlier, has been extensively used in NF over the years and has regained interest recently for RO. One simplified form of the pore flow model assumes that water permeates through membranes as a Hagen–Poiseuille flow through a large array of tortuous cylindrical pores (Fig. 2b), which is the approach adopted in early NF models such as the Donnan steric pore model with dielectric exclusion (DSPM-DE). The Hagen–Poiseuille framework captures water–pore wall friction through the universally assumed no-slip boundary condition, which may obscure differences in water– polymer interactions for different membrane materials. While RO/NF membranes do not have cylindrical pores and a parabolic flow profile is highly questionable for subnanometre pores, the Hagen–Poiseuille framework enables the DSPM-DE to use pore size as an explicit model parameter. In a recent more mechanistic model based on the SF theory (Box 1), steady water flow results from the balance between the driving force for water transport arising from a pressure gradient and the friction between water, the polymer matrix and the moving ions (Fig. 2c). It is worth noting that water and salt transport in the SF model are coupled, with salt transport influencing water transport via water–ion friction. In the context of IEM membranes used in electrodialysis, water–ion friction leads to electro-osmosis, in which an electric-field-driven ion flux results in water flux in addition to the viscous water flow induced by the osmotic pressure difference across the membrane.

### Ion transport

Regardless of specific model, ion transport in membrane processes comprises two steps: partitioning and migration. Ion partitioning refers to events of ions entering or exiting the membrane matrix, whereas migration refers to the process of ions moving across the membrane. In the context of RO/NF and electrodialysis, it is generally accepted that migration is the rate-limiting step and partitioning can thus be approximated as an equilibrium process.

The description of ion transport in the SD model assumes Fickian diffusion for salt transport (Fig. 2d) and relates the salt flux to the transmembrane ion concentration difference using a salt permeability coefficient that accounts for both partitioning and migration. With the water permeability coefficient and salt permeability coefficient, the SD model provides a highly simplified framework for membrane characterization. The framework highlights the trade-off between water–salt selectivity and water permeability and has been widely adopted by the research community. However, the simple SD model has major limitations. The first limitation is that the salt permeability coefficient is not a constant, but is instead dependent on the salt concentration, which limits the applicability of a measured permeability coefficient value when there is a large variation in salt concentration. Second, the SD model does not consider electromigration and is thus not applicable in electrodialysis and NF.

This coefficient implicitly accounts for interrelated mechanisms such as steric exclusion, dielectric exclusion and dehydration, and is typically assumed to be constant for a given membrane–ion pair, independent of feed composition. The Donnan partition coefficient remains explicit and accounts for the impact of salt concentration on partitioning. For ion migration, the SF model unifies diffusion and convection hindrance factors for each ion (in a membrane) into a single frictional factor that depends on the ion–water and ion–membrane friction coefficients (Fig. 2h). The SF model also explains the typically low rejection of small neutral solutes, as Donnan exclusion does not apply to uncharged species, leaving only non-Donnan partitioning effects and frictional interactions to govern their rejection.

The integration of all non-Donnan mechanisms into a single partition coefficient appropriately acknowledges the intrinsic difficulty in quantifying these coupled mechanisms analytically and accurately. Both the non-Donnan partition coefficient and the frictional factor are empirical and do not exhibit the explicit (but probably inaccurate) dependence on ion and pore sizes that is built into the DSPM-DE formulation. Future research can build on the SF model framework to establish more physically grounded relationships between these empirical parameters and measurable ion or membrane properties, and to incorporate additional transport phenomena (for example, ion–ion pairing) not captured by the Nernst–Planck formalism.

## Microscopic behaviour in nano- and ångström-scale channels

### The challenge

Nanofluidics—the study of fluid transport at the nanoscale—has emerged over the past two decades, driven by experimental and instrumental advances that enable the measurement of fluid flows in only a few nanochannels or even a single nanochannel. Such studies have uncovered a remarkable range of unconventional properties that challenge classical principles, from ultrafast flows in carbon nanotubes and slit-like nanochannels, permselective diffusion in artificial water channels and dielectric anomalies under strong confinement to the emergence of quantum crosstalks of fluid flows with their confining structure.

The current challenge is to bridge the gap between these groundbreaking discoveries that relied on nanomaterials to probe water transport at infinitely small length scales and their application at infinitely large scales—reaching thousands of square metres and relying on cost-effective scalable materials with high permeability—as required for membrane-based technologies at the water–energy nexus. This requires careful accounting of mesoscale phenomena and the inherent complexity of real membranes. Features such as structural inhomogeneity or the sparse distribution of functionally active nanodomains make rational innovation in this space particularly challenging.

### Nanofluidics—where are we?

Nanofluidics has been extensively reviewed in recent years. Here we take a broader perspective, focusing on the intimate connections between nanofluidics and membrane science. While fluid dynamics is a 200-year-old field, nanofluidics is only two decades old—enough time to assess its potential. Many key membrane phenomena arise from nanoscale fluid and ion transport through the complex pathways in membrane structures, enabling selective separation, sieving and osmotic effects. The interplay between membrane science and nanofluidics somewhat mirrors polymer science, shifting from engineering-driven applications to a science-focused field and back.

Nanofluidics can be viewed as a sandbox and a collection of model systems that allow emerging phenomena, such as enhanced transport in confinement, Debye-layer behaviour, wall slip and ion selectivity, to be isolated and studied under controlled conditions (Fig. 3). However, we argue that nanofluidics is more than just a sandbox. Nanofluidic phenomena may also point to the need for bold exploration of more exotic transport phenomena that can occur in extreme conditions and regimes that are not encountered in traditional membranes or separation systems. These phenomena, such as extreme confinement, quantum friction, controlled channel solute molecular interactions, strongly correlated transport and dipolar water flow translocation, could point to additional new directions for membrane science to expand beyond the conventional polymeric membrane paradigm in the ongoing pursuit of higher-performance separation technologies.

## Molecular-level mechanisms in membranes and nanochannels

In the absence of experimental techniques that can directly probe membrane transport behaviour at the molecular level, molecular

dynamics (MD) simulations have emerged as indispensable tools for elucidating the mechanisms of water and ion transport. Extensive MD studies on polyamide-based RO and NF membranes have provided valuable insights into nanoscale transport mechanisms and solute– membrane interactions. In contrast, IEMs have received relatively limited attention in MD simulations. Therefore, this section will focus on the molecular-level insights derived from MD studies of polyamide RO and NF membranes.

### Membrane microstructure

Polyamide active layers of thin-film RO and NF membranes are highly crosslinked, nanoporous glassy polymers. MD simulations of these selective layers revealed that the disordered membrane structure contains a broad pore size distribution in the range of 0.3–0.6 nm, consistent with positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy measurements. Nature Water

Rather than well-defined nanochannels, the membrane free volume exists as a maze of irregular cavities and interconnected tortuous pathways. While many of these pores seem to be isolated at equilibrium, MD studies reveal a dynamic polymer matrix in which the thermal motion of the crosslinking skeleton causes pores to continuously open, close and merge on nanosecond timescales (Fig. 4a).

MD simulations also highlight how the connectivity of these pores can evolve under an applied pressure. Under high transmembrane pressure, the polymer matrix undergoes slight compression (compaction), depending on its Young’s modulus, reducing the total porosity (Fig. 4b). Interestingly, MD simulations indicate that pressure can also increase transient pore connectivity: adjacent nanometric voids that were previously disconnected become momentarily linked as the polymer segments shift. In a recent non-equilibrium MD study, applying 300–600 bar of pressure caused a minor

reduction in the fractional free volume, while new percolation pathways appeared throughout the membrane thickness (Fig. 4c). Thus, the membrane microstructure ‘breathes’ under pressure, compressing slightly but also rearranging to create interconnected channels. This dynamic microstructure may explain why polyamide membranes maintain water permeance even as they are compressed under operating pressures.

### Water transport mechanisms

Classical transport models long presumed that water transports through polyamide by an SD mechanism, driven by a water concentration gradient. MD simulations are now overturning this view, instead revealing a pressure-driven viscous flow mechanism for water transport. Recently, non-equilibrium MD studies applied a hydraulic pressure across realistic crosslinked polyamide membranes and directly observed the behaviour of water inside the active layer. These simulations showed that under steady-state water flux, the water concentration profile remains uniform across the membrane thickness (Fig. 4d), whereas the hydrostatic pressure drops linearly from the feed to permeate side (Fig. 4e). In other words, there is an internal pressure gradient pushing water through—a finding in marked contrast to the classical SD model. Notably, MD simulations directly reveal that water molecules move through the membrane as clusters, rather than as isolated single molecules.

As water permeation in polyamide is pressure-gradient driven, the relationship between water flux and applied pressure is fundamentally linear, analogous to Darcy’s law for viscous flow through a porous medium (Fig. 4f). MD simulations covering a wide pressure range (300−1,500 bar) find that permeate water flux increases linearly with pressure difference. The continued linearity at extreme pressures underscores that water transport is limited by frictional resistance within the pores, rather than by a concentration-driven thermodynamic limit.

The pressure-driven pore flow mechanism revealed by MD has been supported by experiments as well. For example, permeation tests with various solvents (water and organic molecules) showed that the solvent flux correlates with the membrane pore size and the solvent size and viscosity, inconsistent with the SD model. Moreover, a recent study extended these MD insights to organic solvent NF membranes, demonstrating that even in non-aqueous systems, flow is still driven by an internal pressure gradient and solvent molecules migrate as clusters through interconnected pathways in the polymer membrane. Together, these findings mark a paradigm shift in our understanding: rather than diffusing as individual molecules (as assumed in the SD model), water in RO/NF membranes moves via pressure-driven flow through pore networks. This mechanistic understanding is prompting the development of new transport models and suggests that efforts to enhance water permeability in polymer membranes should focus on managing the pore network connectivity and water–polymer friction, rather than simply increasing the polymer water solubility.

### Ion transport mechanisms

MD simulations have been equally instrumental in unravelling how salts (ions) traverse, or are rejected, by polyamide RO and NF membranes. At the molecular scale, ion transport is governed by the need for partial dehydration of the ion and specific ion–pore interactions, which together create substantial energy barriers to ion transport. Before an ion can enter a nanopore, it must shed a portion of its hydration shell (that is, the tightly bound water molecules surrounding it in solution) (Fig. 4g(i)). This dehydration process is energetically costly, and MD studies confirm that it is the primary barrier that limits ions from partitioning into the membrane. The free energy required to strip away enough water molecules for the ion to fit into a subnanometre cavity, on the order of tens of kilocalories per mole for typical salts, largely determines whether the ion can even enter the membrane. The Nature Water

Once an ion successfully enters a membrane pore, the next challenge is navigating the intrapore environment. Here, specific interactions with membrane functional groups play a key role in ion mobility (the migration barrier). Polyamide active layers contain charged and polar sites (for example, deprotonated carboxylate groups, protonated amines and amide linkages) that can interact with a partially dehydrated ion. MD studies reveal that ions that form strong coordinative or electrostatic interactions with these sites tend to get ‘stuck’, and their migration through the pore is hindered due to frequent binding events with the polymer matrix (Fig. 4g(ii)). These frequent binding events manifest as friction and the well-observed hopping-diffusion phenomena. In contrast, ions that interact only weakly with the pore walls can transport more freely, and consequently have less friction. This gives rise to a trade-off: ions that are more easily dehydrated tend to exhibit lower entry barriers but stronger interactions within the pore, leading to higher migration barriers. For instance, Cl − ions that dehydrate more readily show a higher partitioning coefficient than F − at the pore entrance, whereas once inside, their diffusion coefficient becomes lower than that of F − (Fig. 4g(iii)). MD simulations of sulfonated polymer pores have also shown that Li⁺, despite its higher surface charge density, has a weaker binding affinity to sulfonate sites due to its thicker hydration shells, allowing it to hop through more readily, whereas Na⁺ or K⁺, which undergo greater dehydration within membranes, bind more strongly and thus move more slowly. This counterintuitive behaviour underscores that ion selectivity in membranes arises from a balance between dehydration and specific ion–membrane interactions.

In RO membranes, these molecular mechanisms result in extremely low ion permeabilities (typically 4–8 orders of magnitude lower than that of water), as observed in both MD simulations and experiments. The subnanometre pores of the polyamide membranes are so tight that monovalent ions such as Na⁺ or Cl⁻ must lose most of their hydration shell to pass, an event that is statistically rare under ambient pressures. MD simulations have calculated that at normal operating pressures (50–80 bar), the probability of a fully hydrated Na⁺ entering a 0.5 nm pore is essentially zero; only if the ion partially dehydrates (losing 2–3 water molecules) can it transiently fit through a ‘gap’ in the polymer matrix. Even then, the ion often forms a complex with a carboxylate group at the entry, effectively raising the local energy barrier further by requiring dissociation to resume its journey. This is a molecular picture of ion exclusion: the membrane acts as an energy filter that lets water through but forces ions to climb a steep free energy hill (dehydration plus dissociation) to permeate. Divalent ions (for example, Ca²⁺ and SO₄²⁻) face an even steeper hill due to their higher charge and hydration energy, explaining the strong preference of RO and NF membranes for rejecting multivalent salts over monovalent ones. Overall, MD simulations provide a detailed molecular picture of ion transport in RO and NF membranes: ions must partially shed their hydration shells and then contend with binding/unbinding to internal sites of the membrane as they travel through ångström-scale pores.

### Limitations of MD simulations

Classical MD simulations provide valuable atomic-level insights into membrane transport, but they are inherently limited in both spatial and temporal scales. Typically, MD can access only nanometre length scales and up to microseconds in time. As a result, slow processes (such as ion transport) or large-scale phenomena (such as the spatial heterogeneity of polymer membranes) may be missed. In addition, most MD force fields employ fixed-charge, pairwise-additive potentials that cannot capture electronic polarization, bond breaking and formation or complex charge-transfer effects. As a result, certain interactions (for example, charge-induced dipoles and changes in protonation) are neglected, potentially biasing the ion transport rate and selectivity of membranes. Finally, results from a tiny periodic cell can deviate from macroscopic behaviour: sampling fluctuations or boundary effects in a small MD box may not represent the bulk system. Together, these limitations imply that MD predictions should be interpreted with caution when applied to real-world membrane systems. Notably, integrating MD with machine learning offers opportunities to overcome these limitations and better investigate membrane transport (Box 3).

Bridging the gap between molecular, microscopic and macroscopic scales is essential for developing a true mechanistic understanding of Nature Water

water and ion transport in membranes. Current macroscopic models oversimplify membrane pore structure and interactions, while molecular and nanofluidic studies capture detailed atomic- and nanoscale mechanisms that are difficult to translate to real membranes. Overcoming this barrier will be critical for translating molecular-level design principles into next-generation, high-performance membrane technologies.

## Outlook

In this Perspective, we integrated current understanding from molecular, microscopic and macroscopic investigations to establish a mechanistic framework for solute and water transport in membranes. Bridging these multiple scales is essential to uncover the governing principles of transport in polymer membranes and to provide a scientific basis for designing high-performance membranes for applications in desalination, water purification, resource recovery and technologies at the water–energy nexus. Specifically, we analysed existing macroscopic models, emphasizing their simplifying assumptions and inherent limitations; examined insights from the fields of nano- and angstrofluidics that can shed light on transport mechanisms within more complex membrane structures; highlighted how molecular simulations offer atomistic resolution for understanding the transport of water and ions across the heterogeneous porous networks of membranes; and discussed the need to integrate these molecular, microscopic and macroscopic perspectives to advance theoretical understanding and guide the rational design of high-performance membranes. While our discussion centred on RO/NF desalination membranes, the multiscale principles outlined here are broadly applicable to other aqueous separation membranes, including ion-exchange membranes for electrodialysis, in which similar fixed-charge effects, ion–membrane interactions and frictional forces critically influence ion and water transport.

Current state-of-the-art frameworks for modelling ion transport in membranes face substantial limitations that constrain their predictive power and broad applicability. These models rely on simplified mechanistic descriptions of ion partitioning—such as steric, Donnan and dielectric exclusions—that fail to fully capture the underlying physics. For instance, dehydration, a key ion partitioning mechanism, is not explicitly modelled but is instead conflated with the steric or Nature Water

Nano- and angstrofluidics provide controlled platforms where transport can be probed at molecular resolution, revealing effects such as ion dehydration barriers, dielectric exclusion and interfacial friction that classical continuum models cannot capture. These insights bridge the gap between atomistic physics and membrane engineering, quantifying parameters such as wall–fluid friction, hydration penalties and effective dielectric constants that inform predictive models. However, applying these parameters to real membranes is a substantial challenge. Practical membranes have complex and tortuous pore networks with critical collective interactions, pore connectivity and mesoscale phenomena (such as concentration polarization) that are not present in simplified nanofluidic systems. In addition, major research gaps remain even in nanofluidics research; current studies rely on measurements of global transport rates, leaving us essentially blind to the complicated dynamics that take place inside membrane channels. Future work must advance operando characterization tools, such as super-resolution imaging, ultrafast microscopy, spin-based techniques, and single-molecule and sum-frequency spectroscopies, to achieve spatial, chemical and temporal resolution of chemical processes inside pores. Most experiments treat membrane pores as a black box between an entrance and an exit; however, we need a better understanding of the local concentration, hydration, ion partitioning and local conductance mapping inside subnanometre pores. Nanofluidics must also address the complexity of real membranes by developing methods to handle heterogeneity, matrix dynamics and asymmetry. Real-world separations rarely occur at equilibrium, and nanofluidic studies have already demonstrated strongly nonlinear transport phenomena. The membrane field also needs to embrace this paradigm and harness far-from-equilibrium and active transport processes (for example, those in the kidney) to enhance separation efficiency and selectivity. Looking ahead, integrating these molecular insights with the development of scalable architectures, novel materials and dynamically adaptable membranes will be key to bridging the gap from model channels to real-world membranes. In this way, nano- and angstrofluidics will evolve from a sandbox for fundamental discoveries to a driving force in the rational design of next-generation, high-performance membrane technologies.

MD simulations are now indispensable for probing water and ion transport through membranes, but bridging the gap to real process scales remains a frontier. Serving as an atomistic ‘computational microscope’, MD simulations reveal transport phenomena that are otherwise

beyond the reach of experimental observation. MD studies have elucidated important phenomena, such as ultra-fast, low-friction water flow in carbon nanotubes, screw-like molecular motion in confined channels and the energetic penalties ions incur when shedding hydration shells to pass through tight pores. However, despite these successes, MD still faces significant limitations that must be overcome to fully realize its potential. Current simulations typically span only nanosecond to microsecond timescales and length scales of tens of nanometres, far from the second- to hour- and micrometre-scale dimensions relevant to real membrane processes. Bridging this vast gap in scales will require accelerated simulation techniques, coarse-grained modelling methods and ML-augmented approaches capable of capturing rare events and structural heterogeneity of membranes. Another frontier lies in modelling realistic feed water chemistries. Most MD studies have focused on simple single-salt solutions, leaving the behaviour of mixed solutes—including competitive ion transport and interactions with organic molecules—poorly understood. Future studies should also address competitive transport in mixed-ion or multi-solute systems, extending these mechanisms to more complex, real-world water chemistry. Furthermore, the validation of MD predictions demands closer integration with advanced experimental characterization techniques. Developing operando techniques to probe hydrogen-bond networks, local stress distributions and water structuring inside membranes will be crucial to verify and refine insights from simulations. Finally, translating atomic-scale insights from MD into tangible membrane designs remains a formidable challenge. While MD points to strategies such as tuning the pore entrance chemistry or modulating dehydration barriers, synthesizing membranes with ångström-level precision remains beyond current fabrication capabilities. Addressing these gaps will require a concerted effort. Future research must integrate state-of-the-art MD simulations, multiscale modelling and targeted experimental validation to pave the way for predictive membrane design and optimized separation performance.

Physics-informed ML represents a promising avenue for unifying mechanistic understanding and data-driven modelling in membrane science. For example, a recent study developed a physics-informed deep learning model for multi-species ion transport in polyamide membranes by embedding classical closure relations as inductive biases within a neural differential equation framework. The hybrid model, pretrained on continuum-model simulations and fine-tuned with experimental data, captures complex ion-pairing interactions and shows strong agreement with measurements, outperforming traditional partial-differential-equation-based solvers in predictive accuracy. Such advances illustrate how physics-informed ML can overcome the limitations of conventional transport models by encoding physical laws into flexible learning architectures, paving the way towards more robust, interpretable and predictive tools for membrane design.

Recent multiscale and enhanced modelling strategies are bridging the gap between atomistic MD and macroscopic membrane transport. Machine-learned force fields trained on electronic-structure data capture polarization and bond reactivity with an accuracy close to that of density functional theory at a fraction of the cost, enabling quantum-level MD of thousands of atoms over nanoseconds to probe complex liquids and materials with impressive fidelity. Coarse-grained MD groups atoms into effective beads to dramatically accelerate dynamics and access microsecond timescales and mesoscale behaviour, while emerging ML approaches such as CGnets systematically refine coarse potentials to preserve essential structural and thermodynamic trends. At even larger scales, lattice Boltzmann methods and related continuum solvers efficiently capture hydrodynamics, pressure gradients and collective transport, and can be hybridized with MD or coarse-grained MD to simulate flows and membrane processes far beyond the reach of standalone MD. Bridging the gap between macroscopic, microscopic and molecular scales remains the central challenge in advancing our understanding of water and solute transport in state-of-the-art polymer membranes. One promising direction is the integration of insights from molecular simulations and nanofluidic studies to design membranes composed of well-defined channels in which transport properties are quantitatively understood. Such model membranes would allow the systematic exploration of pore–pore correlations, heterogeneity and collective transport effects that are largely inaccessible in current polymeric systems. ## BOX 1 · Mechanism of water transport in RO and NF membranes

The traditional SD model has been the predominant framework to describe water transport in RO membranes for the past half-century. The model assumes that water first dissolves into a ‘dense’ non-porous polymer membrane and then diffuses through the membrane down a water concentration gradient. One of the key assumptions in the SD model is a constant pressure profile within the membrane, with a sharp drop at the membrane–permeate interface (Fig. 2a).

However, recent research advances, including experimental findings and molecular simulations, have shown that the SD model fails to capture the true underlying mechanism of water transport in RO membranes. Advanced characterization techniques, such as positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy, small-angle neutron scattering and wide-angle X-ray scattering, confirm the presence of subnanometre pores and interconnected pore networks in polyamide RO membranes, and MD simulations reveal a microporous structure with interconnected subnanometre channels or pores. MD simulations also reveal that water molecules are clustered within these pores and move collectively as a viscous flow driven by a pressure gradient (Fig. 2b,c), rather than diffusing as dispersed molecules along a water concentration gradient.

The SF model has recently been established to describe the coupled transport of water and salt in RO and NF membranes. In this friction model, the permeate water flux ( v w) is governed by a force balance between the pressure gradient driving force and the friction resisting water flow:

## BOX 2 · Angstrofluidics: the molecular-level frontier of membrane transport

Angstrofluidics is an emerging field that investigates fluid and ion transport through channels with characteristic dimensions on the order of a few ångströms, comparable to the size of water molecules and hydrated ions. These length scales represent the extreme limit of fluid confinement, where classical continuum assumptions fail and molecular-level interactions govern transport. While initially explored through model systems such as carbon nanotubes and 2D material slits, angstrofluidics is now recognized as directly relevant to membrane separations: RO and NF membranes operate within this very regime, with recent experimental and computational studies revealing subnanometre pores in polyamide membranes. These parallels make angstrofluidics a unique testbed for probing the molecular physics of transport.

### Ion exclusion in confinement

In ångström-scale pores, selectivity is no longer governed by size exclusion alone. Ions experience an energy barrier to pore entry that arises from multiple factors: dehydration from the partial/ complete removal of hydration shells, the ion core diameter and its charge, interface effects from van der Waals and/or specific interactions with the pore walls and the dielectric penalty associated with the low permittivity of solvents in confinement. These effects, which are well optimized for the highest ion specificity in biological channels, collectively yield ion-specific exclusion beyond that predicted by classical models to offer a mechanistic basis for the high salt rejection observed in polymer membranes with subnanometre free-volume elements.

### Water transport and friction

Water confined within ångström-scale channels exhibits highly ordered structures, often forming single-file chains or layered motifs. In this regime, permeability is no longer limited by bulk viscosity, but is instead limited by friction at the solid–liquid interface, which is sensitive to wall chemistry, charge and polarizability. In carbon-based or atomically smooth slit pores and nanotubes, small surface friction dramatically enhances water flows, offering design routes to high-permeability membranes. This hydrodynamic behaviour in ångström-scale channels corroborates the recent conclusion that water transport in RO and NF, which possess ångström-scale interconnected pore networks, is governed by a pressure-driven viscous flow.

### Relevance to membrane science

Even though real membranes exhibit structural and functional complexity well beyond that of model angstrofluidic channels, this framework still can provide a physically grounded basis to understand and quantify the behaviour of water and ions in polymer membranes beyond empirical or semi-continuum models. It offers insight into: • Ion rejection via steric, charge and dielectric exclusion, as well as

dehydration barriers • Water permeability governed by interfacial friction • Asymmetric or nonlinear transport, including rectifying or gating

Moreover, angstrofluidics enables the experimental determination of meaningful transport parameters, such as wall–fluid friction, hydration penalties or effective dielectric constants, that can feed into improved macroscopic models of membrane performance.

Connection to rational membrane design. While real membranes

remain structurally complex and heterogeneous, angstrofluidic systems may serve as well-controlled platforms for isolating dominant transport mechanisms. They provide the missing link between atomistic transport physics and practical membrane engineering, laying the foundation for the next generation of high-performance separation membranes, where selectivity and permeability are tuned at the molecular level.

## BOX 3 · Integrating MD and artificial intelligence/machine learning for membrane transport and design

MD simulations compute Newton’s equations for every atom (water, ions and polymer), yielding an atom-by-atom ‘movie’ of membrane transport. With validated force fields, MD provides a physics-based model of nanoscale behaviour. This computational microscope reveals molecular details that experiments cannot directly observe. For example, non-equilibrium MD (NEMD) combined with experiments showed that water in polyamide RO membranes permeates as transient clusters driven by a hydraulic pressure gradient, not by simple diffusion as once thought. Similarly, MD of charged NF membranes reveals how polymer charge and pore architecture control ion selectivity: a higher negative charge of the membrane (for example, at higher pH) enhances NaCl rejection, and the arrangement of charged groups governs how counterions and co-ions permeate. These atomistic insights, such as water clustering and ion hydration changes, match experimental trends and help explain macroscopic water flux and ion transport that would otherwise be mysterious.

Because MD is computationally demanding, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) tools are increasingly being incorporated to accelerate and extend simulations. ML models can learn from MD (and experimental) data to predict outcomes far more quickly. The influence of input features on the predicted separation factors was analyzed by using the Shapley values. Shapley values quantify the feature’s contribution to the predicted separation factor, as shown in the hierarchy in the SHAP summary plot (Figure 3D). Specifically, the color in the plot represents the value of the input feature, ranging from low (blue) to high (red). For example, the blue color represents a low temperature, while the red color indicates a high temperature. The absolute SHAP value denotes the contribution of the input feature with a higher absolute value representing a greater contribution. A positive Shapley value indicates that an increase in the input feature is associated with a higher predicted parameter. Conversely, a negative Shapley value implies that an increased input feature is associated with a lower predicted parameter. A higher feature value with a higher predicted parameter indicates a positive correlation and vice versa.

Figure 3D presents the SHAP summary plot for the predicted separation factor. Among all of the input features, organic molar volume ranks first and shows a positive correlation with SHAP values. This indicates that a larger organic molar volume is associated with a higher predicted separation factor (Figure 3E). In the PV data set, it is observed that H 2 O has a relatively small molar volume (18.07 cm mol −), while most organic solutes have larger molar volumes (e.g., MeOH with 40.49 cm mol −, acetic acid with 57.25 cm mol −). Therefore, the permeation rate of organic solutes from water is expected to be higher for solutes with smaller molar volumes compared to those with larger molar volumes. Consequently, the selectivity would be higher for organic solutes with larger molar volumes. The mass ratio ( A /B) ranks second (Figure 3F), and in agreement with our previous study, a positive correlation is observed. This means that a higher mass ratio is associated with a higher separation factor. In the context of organic dehydration, a lower ratio of H 2 O in the feed corresponds to a higher selectivity for H 2 O, which aligns with experimental observations. Typically, as the water saturation in the feed solution decreases, the water solubility and diffusivity tend to increase. This can lead to increased intermolecular friction and reduced water permeation during transport through the membrane. Consequently, when the feed solution has a higher concentration of organic solute (a larger mass ratio), water permeation through the membrane becomes more pronounced. In the case of organic extraction, as the concentration of organic solutes increases, they exhibit stronger sorption interactions with the hydrophobic membrane. This increased interaction causes the membrane to swell further, resulting in the creation of free volume and mobility within the membrane. Consequently, the diffusion rate of both

organic solutes and H 2 O are enhanced. However, the diffusion rate of H 2 O increases more significantly due to its smaller molecular diameter (e.g., 0.26 nm for H 2 O vs 0.52 nm for ethanol). As a result, the selectivity decreases.

In general, similar results, albeit with relatively lower accuracy, were obtained for flux prediction compared to the separation factor prediction. Among the 40 different studies in the testing data set, the discrepancy in data positions for on different studies seems to be more significant for total flux than for the separation factor. For example, data from one study July (hexagons in wine color on the right side) exhibited almost a 2026 vertical line, suggesting minor changes in the total flux with substantial differences in experimental conditions (causing different predicted values). Data from another study (triangles in light blue on the left side) showed an opposite pattern as a horizontal line, indicating that the model is not sensitive to the differences in the experimental conditions explored in that study. The model was also found to be overoptimized (e.g., R > 0.85) and less robust when data splitting with strict DLM was not integrated.

In Figure 4D, the experimental temperature ranked first among all input features and demonstrated a positive linear correlation with J (Figure 4E), indicating that higher

polymerization points. temperatures could result in higher total flux. The increase in temperature enhances the driving force for mass transfer due to the higher partial pressure and increased diffusion coefficient of a given solute. − Furthermore, thermal agitation, characterized by the frequency and amplitude of polymer chain movement, also increases with the temperature. This leads to the expansion of the free volume within the membrane and facilitates solute diffusion. The selective layer thickness ranked second and showed a negative correlation with the J (Figure 4F), which aligns with previous studies and is consistent with theoretical models such as Fick’s equation and the solution-diffusion model.

The mass ratio ranked third and exhibited a negative correlation with J (Figure 4G). In the context of both organic dehydration and organic extraction, a higher feed concentration (lower mass ratio) indicates that more target molecules come into contact with the selective layer of the membrane. Consequently, due to membrane selectivity, more target molecules are adsorbed to the membrane, causing greater swelling in the top layer of the membrane. This allows more target molecules to pass through the swollen membrane, leading to increased permeability with higher feed content. The effective area is ranked fourth (Figure 4H) and has a negative correlation with J. This means that, similar to the separation factor, increasing the effective area is not favorable for achieving a higher total flux. The relatively low total flux observed for membranes with larger effective areas may be attributed to the fact that the fabrication of advanced membranes requires novel technologies that have not yet been proven scalable. Also, bench-scale results are often obtained under different operating conditions pilot- and commercial-scale results; in particular, larger module areas can result in reduced driving force along the length of the module. Thus, membrane scale-up should be a key research focus to improve PV membrane selectivity and permeability. Additionally, the filler concentration ranked fifth and exhibited a positive correlation with J. This suggests that the addition of fillers could facilitate solute permeation, as the thickness of

composite membranes is typically smaller than that of homogeneous membranes or possibly due to the defects caused by membrane fabrication with fillers.

Despite the model accuracy, conducting SHAP on a model with DLM would be more reliable than a model without DLM since the model developed with DLM is more robust. Besides, it is worth noting that the SHAP analysis of the currently established models could be limited to drawing conclusions about feature importance. In addition, based on its nature, SHAP explains the importance of features based on ML models instead of real-life PV performance.
